Semi-Minor Axis (b) in the Context of the MPCORB Database

Scientific Definition, Dynamical Interpretation, and Parameter Derivation from the IAU Minor Planet Center Orbit Catalog

1. Fundamental Astrodynamical Definition

In celestial mechanics and analytical geometry, the semi-minor axis, denoted by the symbol b, is a derived geometric parameter of a bound elliptic Keplerian trajectory (0 ≤ e < 1). It represents one-half of the minor axis—the shortest diameter of the orbital ellipse, which passes through the geometric center of the ellipse perpendicular to the line of apsides (the major axis connecting perihelion and aphelion).

Unlike the semi-major axis (a) and the orbital eccentricity (e), which belong to the canonical set of six primary osculating Keplerian orbital elements, the semi-minor axis b is a secondary (derived) orbital parameter uniquely determined by a and e:

b = a √ 1 − e2 = √ q · Q

where q = a(1 − e) is the perihelion distance and Q = a(1 + e) is the aphelion distance. Remarkably, the second identity demonstrates that the semi-minor axis is the exact geometric mean of the perihelion and aphelion distances (whereas the semi-major axis a = (q + Q)/2 is their arithmetic mean). For a strictly circular orbit (e = 0), the semi-minor axis degenerates to the orbital radius (b = a = r).

2. Physical and Dynamical Significance

While the semi-major axis a governs the specific orbital energy and sidereal period of a minor planet, the semi-minor axis b is directly tied to the specific relative angular momentum (h), the areal velocity (dA/dt), and the spatial cross-section of the orbital ellipse.

2.1 Specific Orbital Angular Momentum and Semi-Latus Rectum

In the heliocentric two-body problem, the magnitude of the specific orbital angular momentum vector h = r × v is related to the semi-latus rectum p = a(1 − e2) via h = √(μp). Because b = a√(1 − e2), the semi-minor axis scales linearly with specific angular momentum for a fixed semi-major axis (i.e., fixed orbital energy):

h = √ μ a (1 − e2) = b √ μ a   ⟺   b = h √ a μ

where μ = G(M☉ + m) ≈ GM☉ is the heliocentric gravitational parameter. Furthermore, the total geometric area enclosed by the orbital ellipse is Aorb = πab, yielding an areal velocity of dA/dt = πab/P = h/2 (Kepler's Second Law).

2.2 Time-Averaged Distance with Respect to True Anomaly

Whereas the semi-major axis a represents the mean heliocentric distance averaged over the eccentric anomaly E, the semi-minor axis b equals the mean heliocentric distance averaged uniformly over the true anomaly (ν):

⟨r⟩ν = 1 2π ∫02π r(ν) dν = a √ 1 − e2 = b

3. Specific Status and Derivation in the MPCORB Catalog

The MPCORB (Minor Planet Center Orbit) database, published by the International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center (IAU MPC), stores the primary osculating heliocentric orbital elements for all numbered and unnumbered minor planets. With respect to the semi-minor axis b, the following catalog conventions apply:

  • Not Explicitly Tabulated in MPCORB.DAT: To avoid redundancy and preserve fixed-width storage efficiency, the semi-minor axis b is not stored as an explicit column in MPCORB.DAT. Instead, it is computed deterministically from two explicitly tabulated osculating elements: the orbital eccentricity (e, stored in columns 71–79) and the semi-major axis (a, stored in columns 93–103).
  • Osculating Heliocentric Nature: Because b is derived directly from the osculating values of a and e at the record's reference Epoch (columns 21–25), b is likewise an osculating heliocentric quantity referred to the J2000.0 mean ecliptic frame. Gravitational perturbations from major planets (notably secular Kozai–Lidov and Laplace–Lagrange eccentricity oscillations) cause both e and b to evolve over time.
  • Unit of Measurement: Because a is tabulated in Astronomical Units (AU) and e is dimensionless, the derived semi-minor axis b is also expressed in Astronomical Units (AU) (1 AU ≡ 149,597,870,700 m).
Domain Restriction (0 ≤ e < 1): As a real-valued Euclidean geometric length, the semi-minor axis b is strictly defined only for closed elliptic orbits (0 ≤ e < 1, where 0 < b ≤ a). At the parabolic boundary (e = 1), b diverges to infinity; for hyperbolic orbits (e > 1, where a < 0), the conjugate semi-axis is defined instead as b = |a|√(e2 − 1), representing the impact parameter of the asymptote relative to the focus.

4. Fixed-Width Source Columns in MPCORB.DAT

To extract the inputs required to calculate b from a standard 202-character MPCORB.DAT record, parse the following fixed-width fields (1-based column indexing):

Parameter Columns (1-based) Python Slice (0-based) Fortran Format Unit & Role
Epoch 21–25 line[20:25] A5 (Packed) Reference epoch for osculating elements
e (Eccentricity) 71–79 line[70:79] F9.7 Dimensionless (0 ≤ e < 1); input for b
a (Semi-major axis) 93–103 line[92:103] F11.7 Astronomical Units (AU); input for b
b (Semi-minor axis) Derived (not stored) a * math.sqrt(1 - e**2) Computed Astronomical Units (AU)

Example Record Extraction (00001 — (1) Ceres)

In the MPCORB record for (1) Ceres, columns 71–79 and 93–103 provide e and a directly: 

Col:  ... 71      79 ... 93       103 ...
Data: ... 0.0785354  ... 2.7653485    ...
          └───┬───┘      └────┬────┘
              e             a (AU)

Computation:
  b = 2.7653485 * sqrt(1 - 0.0785354^2) = 2.7568065 AU

5. Applications in Small-Body Orbital Dynamics

Although a and e are standard in catalog listings, computing the semi-minor axis b is essential in several astrodynamical and observational contexts:

  • 2D Orbital Projection & Visualization: Rendering accurate planar or 3D Keplerian orbit plots requires both semi-axes (a and b) to parameterize the ellipse in the orbital plane via x = a(cos E − e) and y = b sin E.
  • Collisional & Meteoroid Stream Modeling: In Öpik-type collision probability estimators and meteoroid stream dispersion models, the orbital cross-sectional area (πab) and specific angular momentum (h ∝ b/√a) govern encounter geometry and spatial density along the orbital torus.
  • Secular Perturbation Theory: Under conservative planetary perturbations (where a remains secularly invariant to first order), variations in eccentricity e directly modulate b, reflecting angular momentum exchange between the minor planet and the perturbing giant planets.
