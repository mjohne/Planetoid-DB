Mean Daily Motion

Orbit-Averaged Angular Rate and Keplerian Propagation Parameter in MPCORB.DAT

1. Formal Definition

In heliocentric celestial mechanics and the Minor Planet Center Orbit Database (MPCORB), the mean daily motion (n, frequently abbreviated simply as mean motion) is the constant, orbit-averaged angular rate at which a minor planet completes one full sidereal revolution of 2π radians (360°) around the Sun, expressed in degrees per ephemeris day (deg/d, where 1 d = 86,400 s of Terrestrial Time / Barycentric Dynamical Time).

Equivalently, n represents the uniform angular velocity of a fictitious auxiliary body moving along a concentric circular orbit of radius equal to the minor planet's osculating semimajor axis a, completing one revolution in the exact same sidereal period P. Consequently, n serves as the constant time derivative of the mean anomaly M(t) in unperturbed two-body propagation:

n = dM / dt = 2π / P  [rad/d] = 360° / P  [deg/d] (Eq. 1)

2. Mathematical Formulation from Kepler's Third Law

In the IAU Minor Planet Center's heliocentric two-body framework, the mean daily motion is coupled directly to the osculating semimajor axis a (in astronomical units, AU) via Kepler's Third Law of Planetary Motion and the Gaussian gravitational constant k:

nrad = √(μ / a3) = k · a−3/2  [rad/d] (Eq. 2)

Converting from radians per day to degrees per day yields the exact standard relation used to populate MPCORB.DAT:

ndeg = (180 / π) · k · a−3/2 ≈ 0.9856076686 · a−3/2  [deg/d] (Eq. 3)

where:

  • k = 0.01720209895 AU3/2 d−1 is the classical Gaussian gravitational constant (defining the heliocentric gravitational parameter μ = GM⊙ = k2 ≈ 2.959122082855911 × 10−4 AU3 d−2).
  • kdeg = (180/π) k ≈ 0.985607668601425 deg AU3/2 d−1 is the Gaussian constant in degrees per day (corresponding to the mean daily motion of a massless particle at a = 1 AU).
  • a is the osculating semimajor axis in AU (stored in MPCORB.DAT columns 93–103).
  • Note that in standard MPC two-body conventions for minor planets, the negligible mass of the asteroid (m ≪ M⊙) is omitted from μ = G(M⊙ + m) ≈ GM⊙.

3. Representation & Usage in MPCORB.DAT

In the fixed-width ASCII format of MPCORB.DAT, the mean daily motion is explicitly tabulated alongside the semimajor axis so that linear mean-anomaly propagation can be executed immediately without repeated floating-point power evaluations:

MPCORB Attribute Specification / Value Astrodynamical Notes
Character Columns Columns 81–91 (1-based) 11-character field formatted as F11.8 (e.g., 0.21410467 for (1) Ceres).
Physical Unit Degrees per ephemeris day (deg/d) Strictly positive (n > 0) for all bound elliptical orbits (0 ≤ e < 1), including retrograde orbits (i > 90°).
Mean Anomaly Propagation M(t) = (M0 + n · (t − t0)) mod 360° M0 is taken from cols 27–35 at epoch t0 (packed date in cols 21–25).
Sidereal Orbital Period (P) P [d] = 360 / n;   P [yr] = (360 / n) / 365.25 For n = 0.21410467 deg/d, P = 1681.42 d ≈ 4.6035 Julian yr.

4. Osculating vs. Proper Mean Motion & Orbital Resonances

  • Osculating Nature: Because MPCORB.DAT lists osculating orbital elements fitted to a specific standard epoch (typically every 200 days at 0h TT), n reflects the instantaneous two-body ellipse tangent to the minor planet's perturbed trajectory at t0. Planetary perturbations (primarily from Jupiter) cause a and n to undergo short-period oscillations over time.
  • Mean-Motion Resonances (MMRs): In dynamical astronomy, the ratio of a minor planet's mean daily motion n to that of a major planet—most notably Jupiter (nJ ≈ 0.083091 deg/d, aJ ≈ 5.204 AU)—defines commensurabilities (n / nJ ≈ p / q). These resonances sculpt the main asteroid belt into the Kirkwood gaps (e.g., 3:1 at n ≈ 0.295 deg/d, a ≈ 2.50 AU; 2:1 at n ≈ 0.166 deg/d, a ≈ 3.28 AU) as well as stable resonant populations such as the Hilda group (3:2) and Jupiter Trojans (1:1).
  • Distinction from Instantaneous Angular Velocity: Unlike the instantaneous true anomaly rate ν̇ = h / r2, which peaks at perihelion and drops at aphelion, n is strictly constant around any given osculating ellipse and depends exclusively on a (independent of eccentricity e and inclination i).
