Linear Eccentricity (c) in the Context of the MPCORB Database

Scientific Definition, Focal-Offset Metric Geometry, Cartesian Perifocal Translation, and Evaluation from MPCORB

1. Fundamental Conic-Geometric & Astrodynamical Definition

Dimensional & Terminological Distinction: In analytical conic geometry and European celestial mechanics (e.g., German lineare Exzentrizität vs. numerische Exzentrizität), the linear eccentricity—universally denoted by c (and occasionally f or ε in geometric texts)—is a dimensional length quantity (expressed in Astronomical Units, AU, in heliocentric astrometry), defined as the Euclidean distance from the geometric center O of a central conic section (ellipse or hyperbola) to either of its two foci F1, F2. By contrast, the numeric eccentricity e = c/a is its dimensionless scale-normalized ratio.

For any bound elliptic Keplerian orbit (0 ≤ e < 1) with semi-major axis a, semi-minor axis b, perihelion distance q, and aphelion distance Q, the linear eccentricity c satisfies the following exact algebraic identities:

c = a · e = √ a2 − b2 = a − q = Q − a = Q − q 2

Physically and geometrically, c quantifies the spatial displacement (focal offset) between the geometric center O of the orbital ellipse and the center of mass of the Sun located at the primary focus F1:

  • Inter-Focal Separation (2c): The total Euclidean distance between the occupied focus F1 (the Sun) and the vacant (empty) geometric focus F2 along the line of apsides is |F1F2| = 2c = 2ae = Q − q.
  • Circular Limit (e = 0 ⇒ c = 0 AU): Both foci coalesce at the geometric center (F1 = F2 = O), so the center of the orbit coincides with the Sun.
  • High-Eccentricity Elliptic Limit (e → 1− ⇒ c → a−): The primary focus F1 approaches the perihelion vertex (q = a − c → 0+), while the vacant focus F2 approaches the aphelion vertex (Q = a + c → 2a−).
  • Parabolic Degeneracy (e = 1): Because a parabola has no finite geometric center (a → ∞), the linear eccentricity c is undefined (infinite) for parabolic trajectories; only the perihelion distance q and focal parameter p = 2q remain finite.

2. Kinematic & Coordinate-Transformation Role in Two-Body Astrodynamics

Beyond static conic geometry, the linear eccentricity c enters directly into Cartesian perifocal state-vector transformations and Keplerian orbital kinematics:

2.1 Cartesian Perifocal Position via Eccentric Anomaly (E)

In the two-dimensional perifocal coordinate system (xω, yω) centered at the primary focus F1 (the Sun), with +xω directed toward perihelion and +yω along the semi-latus rectum at ν = +90°, the heliocentric position of an orbiting body as a function of the eccentric anomaly E is:

xω(E) = a cos E − c  ,    yω(E) = b sin E = a √ 1 − e2 sin E

Here, subtracting the linear eccentricity c = ae translates the origin of the auxiliary circle parametrization (a cos E, b sin E) from the geometric ellipse center O to the physical center of attraction at the Sun (F1). Similarly, the 3D heliocentric position vector of the geometric ellipse center rcenter and of the vacant focus rF2 in J2000.0 ecliptic coordinates are given by:

rcenter = −c P̂ = −a e P̂  ,    rF2 = −2c P̂ = −2a e P̂

where P̂ is the unit vector pointing from the Sun toward perihelion (so the ellipse center and vacant focus both lie on the aphelion side of the Sun along −P̂).

2.2 Radial Velocity & Vis-Viva Coupling

By the vis-viva equation, the heliocentric orbital speed at the geometric minor-axis crossings (r = a, occurring at E = ±π/2 and cos ν = −e = −c/a) equals the circular speed v = √(μ/a), while the radial velocity component ṙ at that point reaches its maximum magnitude governed directly by the ratio c/a:

ṙmax = e √ μ p = c a √ μ p

3. Specific Status and Derivation in the MPCORB Catalog

In the MPCORB (Minor Planet Center Orbit) database published by the International Astronomical Union’s Minor Planet Center (IAU MPC):

  • Implicit Derived Quantity (Not Tabulated): The linear eccentricity c is not stored as an explicit column in MPCORB.DAT. Instead, it is computed directly from the osculating numeric eccentricity e (columns 71–79, format F9.7) and osculating semi-major axis a (columns 93–103, format F11.7) via the product c = a · e.
  • Well-Defined Across the Entire MPCORB.DAT Catalog: Because every record in MPCORB.DAT represents a bound elliptic orbit (0 ≤ e < 1) with finite positive semi-major axis a > 0, the linear eccentricity c is strictly finite, non-negative, and bounded by 0 ≤ c < a for 100% of MPCORB records (with zero division-by-zero singularities, even for strictly circular orbits where e = 0 ⇒ c = 0 AU).
  • Osculating Heliocentric Reference & Unit: Derived from MPCORB elements at the record’s packed Epoch (columns 21–25) in the J2000.0 mean ecliptic frame, c is an instantaneous osculating heliocentric length expressed in Astronomical Units (AU) (1 AU ≡ 149,597,870,700 m).
  • Primary Applications in MPCORB Visualization & Ephemerides:
    • 3D Orbit Rendering & Bounding Boxes: Translating an orbit ellipse from its geometric center to the Sun in heliocentric 3D graphics engines requires shifting the ellipse center by −c P̂.
    • Perifocal Ephemeris Evaluation: Computing heliocentric coordinates via eccentric anomaly E uses xω = a cos E − c directly.
Numerical Stability Note: Always evaluate linear eccentricity via multiplication c = a * e rather than the Pythagorean difference Math.Sqrt(a * a - b * b). For near-circular main-belt asteroids in MPCORB.DAT (where e ∼ 10−4), a2 − b2 suffers severe catastrophic floating-point cancellation, whereas a * e retains full IEEE-754 double-precision accuracy down to e = 0.
