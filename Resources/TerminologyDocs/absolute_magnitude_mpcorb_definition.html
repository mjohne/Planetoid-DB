Absolute Magnitude (H)

IAU Two-Parameter (H, G) Photometric Standard and Size–Albedo Scaling in MPCORB.DAT

1. Formal Definition

In planetary photometry, observational astronomy, and the Minor Planet Center Orbit Database (MPCORB), the absolute magnitude (H) of a solar system minor body (asteroid, centaur, or trans-Neptunian object) is defined as the hypothetical visual (Johnson V-band) apparent magnitude that the object would exhibit if it were situated simultaneously at a heliocentric distance of r = 1 AU, a geocentric (observer) distance of Δ = 1 AU, and at a solar phase angle of α = 0° (idealized zero-phase opposition, where the Sun, observer, and minor planet are collinear with the minor planet fully illuminated).

Because a body cannot physically be observed at r = 1 AU, Δ = 1 AU, and α = 0° at the same time (which would require the observer to coincide with the center of the Sun), H is an extrapolated photometric invariant serving as a logarithmic proxy for the object's intrinsic optical cross-section (pV D2, the product of geometric albedo and projected surface area). In accordance with the IAU 1985 two-parameter (H, G) magnitude system (Bowell et al. 1989), H is formally coupled to the observed apparent visual magnitude V(r, Δ, α) via:

H = V(r, Δ, α) − 5 log10(r · Δ) + 2.5 log10 &left;[ (1 − G) Φ1(α) + G Φ2(α) &right;]  [mag] (Eq. 1)

where:

  • r is the instantaneous heliocentric distance of the minor planet in astronomical units (AU).
  • Δ is the instantaneous topocentric/geocentric distance from the observer to the minor planet in AU.
  • α = arccos((r2 + Δ2 − R⊕2) / (2 r Δ)) is the solar phase angle (Sun–object–observer angle), with R⊕ denoting the Sun–observer distance in AU.
  • G ∈ [0, 1] is the dimensionless slope parameter describing the steepness of the phase curve (tabulated alongside H in MPCORB.DAT; defaulted to G = 0.15 when photometric phase coverage is insufficient).
  • Φ1(α) and Φ2(α) are the normalized basis phase functions satisfying Φ1(0°) = Φ2(0°) = 1, which model the non-linear opposition surge (coherent backscatter and shadow hiding) near α → 0°.

2. The IAU Two-Parameter (H, G) Phase Law

In the IAU 1985 photometric standard adopted across all MPCORB.DAT records, the reduced magnitude H(α) = V(r, Δ, α) − 5 log10(r Δ) varies with solar phase angle α (for 0° ≤ α ≤ 120°) according to:

H(α) = H − 2.5 log10 &left;[ (1 − G) Φ1(α) + G Φ2(α) &right;] (Eq. 2)

The analytical basis functions Φ1(α) and Φ2(α) are parameterized as:

Φi(α) = exp &left;[ −Ai · (tan(α / 2))Bi &right;], &quad; i ∈ {1, 2} (Eq. 3)

with empirically calibrated dimensionless constants A1 = 3.332, B1 = 0.631, A2 = 1.862, and B2 = 1.218. Because the derivative dΦi/dα diverges at α → 0° (B1 < 1), this formulation captures the sharp non-linear brightness spike (the opposition effect, typically 0.2–0.4 mag within α < 7°) observed on regolith-covered airless bodies.

3. Representation & Precision in MPCORB.DAT

In the fixed-width ASCII record format of MPCORB.DAT, the absolute magnitude H and slope parameter G are tabulated immediately following the provisional/numbered designation:

MPCORB Field Character Columns & Format Physical Domain & Catalog Conventions
Absolute Magnitude (H) Columns 9–13 (1-based), width 5 (F5.2) Visual V-band magnitude; spans H ≈ −1.1 ((134340) Pluto) and H = 3.34 ((1) Ceres) to H > 34 (meter-scale NEAs). Blank for pure comets.
Slope Parameter (G) Columns 15–19 (1-based), width 5 (F5.2) Dimensionless; defaults to 0.15 for >99% of objects. Low-albedo C-types exhibit G ≈ 0.05–0.12; moderate S-types G ≈ 0.20–0.25; high-albedo E-types G ≈ 0.40–0.50.
Photometric Bandpass Johnson V-band (λeff ≈ 551 nm) Multi-band astrometric survey photometry (e.g., Gaia G, Pan-STARRS w/r/i, ZTF g/r) is transformed to Johnson V via mean color offsets prior to fitting H.
Typical Uncertainty (σH) ±0.05 mag to ±0.50 mag Dominated by unmodeled rotational lightcurve amplitude, aspect changes, color transformations, and assumed G = 0.15.

4. Physical Relation to Effective Diameter (D) & Geometric Albedo (pV)

For a spherical body of effective diameter D (in kilometers) and visual geometric albedo pV, the absolute magnitude H satisfies the fundamental radiative-balance equation (Fowler & Chillemi 1992; Pravec & Harris 2007):

D = (2 · AUkm · 10V⊙ / 5 / √pV) · 10−H / 5 ≈ (1329 / √pV) · 10−0.2 H  [km] (Eq. 4)

Inverting Eq. 4 expresses the absolute magnitude directly in terms of size and reflectivity:

H = 15.618 − 5 log10(D) − 2.5 log10(pV)  [mag] (Eq. 5)
  • Calibration Constant (1329 km): Derived from 2 × 149,597,870.7 km × 10−26.76 / 5 = 1329.0 km, adopting the apparent visual magnitude of the Sun V⊙ = −26.76 mag.
  • Size–Albedo Degeneracy: From H alone, diameter and albedo are degenerate: a bright, small S-type asteroid (pV ≈ 0.20) has the same H as a dark, carbonaceous C-type asteroid (pV ≈ 0.05) with twice the diameter (DC / DS = √(0.20 / 0.05) = 2).
  • Magnitude–Size Scaling Rules: Each decrease of ΔH = −1.0 mag corresponds to an increase in reflected flux by 100.4 ≈ 2.512 and an increase in diameter by 100.2 ≈ 1.585 at fixed albedo; a decrease of ΔH = −5.0 mag corresponds to a 10× larger diameter (100× greater cross-sectional area).
  • Limitation for Active Comets: Because cometary coma brightness does not follow r−2 illumination scaling (sublimation activity varies roughly as r−n with n ≈ 3–6), comets in the MPC comet table use the total magnitude parameters (M1, K1) rather than (H, G).
