True Anomaly (ν)

Focal Polar Angle, Conic Trajectory Equation & Polar Kinematics in MPCORB.DAT

1. Formal Definition

In celestial mechanics, astrodynamics, and derived orbital parameterizations computed from the Minor Planet Center Orbit Database (MPCORB), the true anomaly (ν, also denoted f or θ) is the actual physical polar angle measured at the Sun (the occupied focus F1 of the osculating conic section) within the minor body's orbital plane between the direction of perihelion (P, defined by the eccentricity vector e) and the instantaneous heliocentric position vector r(t) in the direction of orbital motion:

\cos ν(t) = rac{e · r(t)}{e \, r(t)}, &quad; \sin ν(t) = rac{(h × e) · r(t)}{h \, e \, r(t)}, &quad; ν(t) ∈ [0°, 360°) (Eq. 1)

where:

  • e = rac{1}{\mu}\left[(v2 − rac{\mu}{r})r − (r · v)v ight] is the Laplace–Runge–Lenz eccentricity vector pointing toward perihelion with magnitude e = |e| (MPCORB.DAT, columns 71–79), and h = r × v is the specific relative angular momentum vector.
  • Unlike the mean anomaly M(t) = M0 + n(t − t0) stored in MPCORB.DAT (columns 27–35) or the auxiliary eccentric anomaly E(t), ν(t) is a direct physical angle in three-dimensional space that locates the body on its Keplerian trajectory and governs the polar conic equation r(ν) = rac{p}{1 + e \cos ν}.
  • The difference ν(t) − M(t) is known classically as the equation of the center; for 0 < e < 1 and 0° < M < 180°, Kepler's second law enforces M(t) < E(t) < ν(t) because the body moves fastest near perihelion.
Figure 1: Polar conic geometry of the true anomaly (ν) measured counterclockwise at the Sun from perihelion P to the minor body's position vector r(t). Unlike the eccentric anomaly E (measured at the ellipse center), ν directly sets the heliocentric distance r(ν) = p/(1 + e cos ν), the radial and transverse velocities (vr, vν), and the flight-path elevation angle γ.

2. Derivation from MPCORB.DAT & Exact Conversion Identities

MPCORB.DAT stores the mean anomaly at epoch M0 (columns 27–35), eccentricity e (columns 71–79), mean motion n (columns 81–91), and semimajor axis a (columns 93–103). Obtaining ν(t) at target epoch t proceeds in two stages: (i) advancing M(t) = M0 + n(t − t0) and solving Kepler's equation M(t) = E(t) − e sin E(t) for the eccentric anomaly E(t), and (ii) mapping E(t) to ν(t) via the quadrant-preserving half-angle tangent formula:

ν(t) = 2 \, \mathrm{atan2} &left( √(1 + e) \sin rac{E(t)}{2}, \; √(1 − e) \cos rac{E(t)}{2} &right), \quad \cos ν = rac{\cos E − e}{1 − e \cos E}, \quad \sin ν = rac{√(1 − e2) \sin E}{1 − e \cos E} (Eq. 2)

For low-eccentricity orbits (e ≪ 1), ν(t) can alternatively be expanded directly in terms of M(t) via the classical equation-of-the-center Fourier series:

ν(t) = M(t) + &left(2e − rac{1}{4}e3 + rac{5}{96}e5&right) \sin M(t) + &left( rac{5}{4}e2 − rac{11}{24}e4&right) \sin 2M(t) + &left( rac{13}{12}e3 − rac{43}{64}e5&right) \sin 3M(t) + \mathcal{O}(e4) (Eq. 3)

3. Kinematics, Angular Rate & Cardinal Orbital Points

By Kepler's second law (conservation of specific angular momentum h = r2 \dot{ν} = √{\mu a(1 − e2)} = na2√(1 − e2)), the instantaneous angular velocity dν/dt and polar velocity components (vr, vν) are governed explicitly by ν(t):

rac{dν}{dt} = rac{h}{r2} = rac{n(1 + e \cos ν)2}{(1 − e2)3/2}, \quad vr = \dot{r} = \sqrt{ rac{\mu}{p}} \, e \sin ν, \quad vν = r\dot{ν} = \sqrt{ rac{\mu}{p}} (1 + e \cos ν) (Eq. 4)

ν = 0° (E = M = 0°) Perihelion (P): Closest approach to the Sun r = q = a(1 − e); vr = 0; maximum speed vP = √[\mu(1+e)/(a(1−e))]
ν = 90° (\cos E = e) Semi-latus Rectum (+p): Outbound quadrature to line of apsides r = p = a(1 − e2); maximum outward radial speed vr,max = √(\mu/p) \, e
ν = 180° (E = M = 180°) Aphelion (Q): Farthest recession from the Sun r = Q = a(1 + e); vr = 0; minimum speed vQ = √[\mu(1−e)/(a(1+e))]
ν = 270° (\cos E = e) Semi-latus Rectum (−p): Inbound quadrature to line of apsides r = p = a(1 − e2); maximum inward radial speed vr,min = −√(\mu/p) \, e

4. Astrodynamical & Observational Significance

  • 3D Ephemeris & Argument of Latitude Coupling: Adding ν(t) to the MPCORB.DAT argument of perihelion ω gives the instantaneous argument of latitude u(t) = (ω + ν(t)) &pmod{360°}, which directly determines the minor planet's heliocentric ecliptic coordinates (X, Y, Z) and topocentric right ascension and declination (α, δ) for telescope pointing.
  • Gauss's Planetary Equations (Osculating Element Evolution): Under non-Keplerian perturbations (planetary third-body gravity, Yarkovsky drift, or solar radiation pressure) decomposed into radial (fr), transverse (fν), and normal (fw) components, the osculating rates da/dt, de/dt, and dω/dt depend explicitly on \sin ν and \cos ν:
    rac{da}{dt} = rac{2a2}{h} &left( e \sin ν \, fr + rac{p}{r} fν &right), \quad rac{de}{dt} = rac{1}{h} &left( p \sin ν \, fr + [(p + r)\cos ν + r e] fν &right) (Eq. 5)
  • Circular Orbit Singularity (e = 0): When e = 0, perihelion is undefined and ν becomes indeterminate; in this limit, ν is absorbed into the non-singular argument of latitude u = ω + ν or true longitude Ltrue = Ω + ω + ν.
